Does HOPE's advantage grow with scale?

planned, no code written

HOPE is an architecture from Google Research, published at NeurIPS 2025 in Nested Learning: The Illusion of Deep Learning Architectures. It is two things stacked: a sequence model that learns its own update rule, and a memory system made of blocks that update on different schedules, from very fast to very slow. The authors report that it beats Transformers and modern recurrent models at 760M and 1.3B parameters.

The open question is not whether it works at one size. It is whether the advantage persists as the model grows. Their own table is ambiguous about this, which is what makes it worth measuring.

What the paper's numbers actually show

Transformer++25.2118.5348.6952.25

Read the last row in both directions. On perplexity the gap narrows by 27% going from 760M to 1.3B, which suggests the advantage shrinks as the baseline catches up. On downstream accuracy the gap widens by 39%, which suggests the opposite. Two trends, one table, three times the compute between the points. That is not enough data to tell whether HOPE is a scale story or a small-model story.

The experiment

Rather than argue about it, measure the slope. Train HOPE and a parameter-matched Transformer at several sizes on the same data in the same order, with the same token budget per parameter, and fit a line to the difference between them as a function of log parameters. If the line rises, the mechanism scales. If it is flat or falls, it does not, at least in the range I can reach.

Those times assume about 15 TFLOP/s sustained, which is a guess until measured on the actual card. The rung sizes are provisional: I will fix them after a throughput benchmark, because publishing estimates that turn out wrong is worse than publishing nothing. Two models at every rung, so double the numbers above for the full experiment.

Memory is not the constraint here. 400M parameters in bf16 are 0.8 GB; with fp32 master weights, Adam state, gradients and activations the working set is under 12 GB with gradient checkpointing, and the 64 GB of system RAM absorbs optimizer offload if it gets tight. Compute is what costs, and the largest rung is the only one that is expensive.

What gets measured

  1. Language modelling perplexity on a held-out set, at every rung, both models.
  2. Long-context recall: needle-in-a-haystack at 2k, 4k and 8k, reported with the extra compute the memory blocks consume, not just the accuracy.
  3. Forgetting: two tasks in sequence with no rehearsal. Measure task one before and after task two. This is the claim the architecture is actually built for.

Three seeds at rung one, one seed at rungs two and three, because that is what the budget allows. A result that only appears in one seed at one rung is not a result, and it will be reported as such.

The decision rule, fixed now

If the fitted slope of the accuracy gap is zero or negative across the three rungs, the mechanism does not scale in the range tested and I will say so plainly, with the numbers, and stop. If it is positive but smaller than seed noise at the top rung, the honest conclusion is "unresolved at this scale", not "confirmed". Only a slope that is clearly positive across rungs justifies building the chat product on top.

What this is and is not

It is a scale-trend test between 40M and 400M parameters on a single consumer GPU. It is not a reproduction of the paper's 1.3B results, and it will not settle the question for anyone training at frontier scale. What it can do is resolve the contradiction in their own table, or show that the contradiction is just noise from having two data points.

There is no official implementation. Several public reproductions exist, with different levels of fidelity and at least one disagreement about the update rule; I have listed them below because I will be checking my reading against theirs and saying where I diverge.
