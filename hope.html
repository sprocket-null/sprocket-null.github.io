Does HOPE survive being small?

planned

Google's Nested Learning paper reports a self-modifying architecture called HOPE that beats Transformers at 760M and 1.3B parameters. Nobody has published whether the mechanism still pays for itself at 10–30M parameters on a single 16 GB card. That is a question I can afford to answer, and it is the only claim I'm making.

what the paper actually says

Nested Learning: The Illusion of Deep Learning Architectures (Behrouz, Razaviyayn, Zhong, Mirrokni — NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2512.24695) argues that architecture and optimizer are the same kind of object: a stack of nested optimization problems, each with its own update frequency. Three pieces follow from that:

  • Deep optimizers. Momentum and Adam are associative memories compressing gradients. Replace the dot-product objective with an L2 regression loss and you get a better memory.
  • Self-modifying Titans. A sequence model that learns its own update rule rather than having one fixed by hand.
  • Continuum Memory System (CMS). Instead of short-term vs long-term, a chain of MLP blocks each updating on a different schedule: fast blocks adapt, slow blocks persist. Knowledge can partially recover when it's forgotten.

HOPE is self-modifying Titans plus CMS. The reported numbers, language modelling perplexity and average commonsense accuracy:

what I think is wrong with the popular reading

"Infinite context, infinite reasoning" is not what this is. HOPE's unbounded thing is the number of learning levels it can stack: the model can keep modifying its own update rule, so continual learning doesn't have to end. Context is still finite; the CMS just makes long context cheaper to hold. Reasoning is not infinite and the paper doesn't claim it is.

That distinction matters, because it decides what to measure. If the claim were infinite context, the benchmark would be needle-in-a-haystack at absurd lengths. Since the claim is continual learning without catastrophic forgetting, the benchmark is a sequence of tasks where a normal model decays and this one shouldn't.

the gap I can actually fill

The paper stops at 760M. There is no official code; at least four independent mechanism-level reproductions exist, and none of them publish a compute-matched comparison at small scale. Two things are unknown and cheap to test:

  1. Does CMS beat a param-matched Transformer on long-context recall at equal compute? Not equal tokens — equal wall-clock and equal memory. Multi-frequency blocks cost extra updates per step, and papers usually report the win without the bill.
  2. Does the self-modifying block actually reduce forgetting in a task sequence? Fine-tune task A then task B, no rehearsal, and measure A afterwards. That's the headline claim and it's testable at 10M parameters.

kill criteria, written now

If at matched parameters and matched compute HOPE gains under 2 points of NIAH accuracy and shows no measurable retention advantage across three seeds, I publish the negative result and stop. A small-scale "doesn't transfer" is a useful finding; dressing it up is not.

hardware ceiling

One RTX 5070 Ti, 16 GB. That caps this at tens of millions of parameters, a few hundred million tokens, and days per run. It does not reproduce the paper. It tests whether the mechanism is real at a size where I can run it three times and mean it.

what this is not

  • Not a claim that I can train a frontier model. The gap between 30M and 1.3B is the whole story.
  • Not a product announcement. The chat-facing half is a separate track and may never work.
  • Not a reimplementation of Google's code, because there isn't any. It's my reading of the paper, checked against the public reproductions where they disagree with me.
